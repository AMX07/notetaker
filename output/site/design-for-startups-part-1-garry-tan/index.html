And so at the end of the day, what you're trying to get to is either a prototype or a wireframe.

What these things are are basically—you might use a tool like OmniGraffle or there's quite a few prototyping and wireframing tools that are out there. What you want to do is figure out just the text, just the call to actions, just the flow screen to screen. You don't want to care about color. You want to care about really how it looks, what font you use. You don't even really have to worry about layout too much, though I would start thinking about layout as a part of this process.

And so the most interesting, important aspect of interaction design is actually that people treat—you know, this is kind of a very shocking thing to me. I did research at Stanford with the late professor Cliff Nass, and he built an entire career at Stanford based on this notion that people treat computers like people. And it turns out that if a computer is polite to you, a person will be likewise polite back, or, you know, being negative will sort of elicit the same response. Almost every psychological principle that psychologists have proven, Cliff Nass and Byron Reeves and B.J. Fogg at Stanford were able to show that people mirror with computers even without any sort of anthropomorphism, without an avatar, without anything like that. Truly, every interaction you have with computers is actually a conversation. It's a conversation that happens over and over again because it's written in pixels, it's written in design, it's written in code.

So what is this about at the end of the day? Interaction design is actually about commands. It's about telling people what to do and you actually doing it. People are incredibly suggestible. Actually, one of the most interesting psychological phenomena that I can think of—it maybe explains Trump and a lot of other things—is that when you absorb media, whether it's reading or any type of media, period, you actually read that stuff in your own voice. And so that actually becomes deeper, a part of you. And so as an interaction designer, an interaction designer is not merely the person who figures out where the buttons go or what the layout might be or what the flow is. They're also the writers. They're actually trying to influence people directly by using direct command language.

So this is actually one of the most common mistakes that founders make. They write like this: passive voice. You're not showing, you're telling. And you're just describing something about what you're doing in this third-party, disembodied voice. Command language is about people will do whatever you tell them to do. You just really do have to tell them. And so, you know, this is a super common thing. In fact, I'm pretty sure a lot of you are making this mistake in your homepage copy, in your design, in your first-time experience.

I thought a lot about why this is. I actually think big companies actually can get away with this. If you go to most big company websites, they are actually written in a very passive voice. If you go to Microsoft's home page and start clicking through the products, the product names are like "blah blah blah server edition admin, you know, two thousand and nine," whatever it is—like this insane word salad. And if you read it, you have no idea what that product is and who it's for.

But Microsoft can do that because it's incredibly big and has a scale that's unimaginable. And it has a sales force that will go out and sell their product, and they have incredible amounts of capital. But you as a startup cannot afford that. And so everything that you write needs to be direct. It has to be a direct personal voice, and you need the call to action be incredibly obvious.

The previous—you know, why is that? Why is this so small? This happens all the time, actually. People never are opinionated about what you want the next user to do. And so, what you want is contrast. You want to be incredibly direct. You want to use command language. You want the call to action be obvious. Someone goes to that web page and they just know. They go to that mobile app and they just know what they need to do.

Breaking Complex Actions Into Steps

You know, the other part of interaction design is actually very important—trying to figure out how do you, aside from direct command language, get people to actually do things. So obviously this is an oversimplification, but I generally think about this in two ways. One is how do you remove actions. One of the things that Paul Graham really directly called out to me on our sign up page back in 2008 was, "Why the hell do you have a confirmed password?" Well, I mean, you have their email address. If it's broken, just ask them to click forgot password. It's fine. It's really not that bad to just have them type a wrong password and have them recover it. And most people won't type the wrong password, so it's fine. Why would you optimize for this strange case that doesn't happen that often?

And it's actually really interesting. If you remove that confirmed password, it actually will increase conversion on a sign up flow by as much as fifty percent on average. So it's significant. Cognitive load is an incredibly real issue.

The other strategy that you can really use is how do you chop up an action if it's incredibly complicated? How do you break it up into steps? And so I'd like to use this example because it's like Windows 95 is this absurd—I mean, one of the first wizards really to come out—but I think that there is a timeless aspect to it. The other thing that I often think about, because some of you in this room will be doing fairly B2B enterprise, just like complicated configuration steps, kind of like doing your taxes. Actually, you don't have to do your taxes that often, but when you do it can get really complicated. You might have branching flow, you might have a lot that you have to take care of. And so the other thing that you can do is actually chop up those actions more intelligently with a wizard.

These are a couple of patterns that you can use. And then I want to sort of stop there and really point out another super big misconception that beginning designers or people doing it yourself have—they sort of run into it all the time. They're always trying to do something incredibly novel, and at the interaction design stage, I don't recommend that. I'd actually really recommend that you just steal what works. So you don't reinvent the wheel. It's very important for you to be aware of what are the conventions and things that people already use.

These are very basic ones. For instance, pull to refresh is something that works incredibly well—a lot of apps do it. But it's become a convention because it's easy and natural to use, it's satisfying, it's great to use. Swipe left to right—you know, this is from the Mailbox app, which sold to Dropbox. And so there's a reason why Mailbox was incredibly innovative, but the reality was like you guys generally don't need to. You can get really, really far with just design patterns. And this does take a little bit of time, and frankly anyone who uses computers for any amount of time can just click through all these things and you'll just know.

Visual Design Fundamentals

Synapses of recognition will just come to you. You'll just realize, "Oh my God, there's actually so much you have already seen that you can just steal wholesale." And that's actually what is desirable for most of your designs. You don't want to be novel; you want to be something that gets people to the right place as quickly as possible.

There's just so many of these that I can't possibly cover them like that. It would be its own ten-week course. I'd highly recommend going to that website and checking it out.

One of the really interesting things I do want to call out is sort of a danger zone for using design patterns. One of the more common ones I've seen is using the wrong kind of pagination. One of the things that can be very frustrating to use is when people design for the web and they actually use these little dots to represent where you are in sort of a swiping navigation, but you're on the web using a touchpad, and that makes no sense at all. In fact, it's really, really bad.

You have to be incredibly careful with mixing modalities. Even as you implement each one of these design patterns, you should think through: well, why am I here? What is the user trying to do? And does this make sense for my modality?

Here's probably the most tactical part of the whole talk, but we're going to start off with a little bit of theory. Visual design really is about these things blending together. Interaction design and visual design are super linked. It really is how you tell a user what is important at the visual level—what emotions do we want to evoke, how do we want them to feel? The output of this is either pixel-level compositions, usually in Photoshop, or sometimes the actual HTML/CSS.

And so the really interesting thing here is that to go back to function over form, when you're forced to be simple, you actually have to solve the real problem. So I want to start off by just saying let's avoid as much ornament as possible. Ultimately, we're here to build something that solves people's problems, and it's about that substance.

One of my favorite design thinkers is actually Edward Tufte. He has a great book called "The Visual Display of Quantitative Information." One of the key concepts that he talks about is chart junk. He just can't stand chart junk. These are just examples of it. Here's a chart but there's no y-axis, and we don't know what the x-axis means either. Why is that a green background? For this map, why are these strange gradients and what do they mean?

One of the major misconceptions that people starting out have around visual design is that visual design is not actually about necessarily expressing yourself per se. The key thing is, what information are you trying to get across? One of the key principles that you can actually apply is to look at any design that you're doing and just try to figure out if it can be removed without taking away any meaning. That includes text, lines, borders, really anything. I mean, even colons—a pet peeve of mine is seeing colons all over a form on the web or in a mobile app. If you removed it, you wouldn't even notice it. In fact, it looks cleaner. This is a stupid example but an incredibly basic one that you can apply over and over again throughout anything that you do.

Ornaments at the end of the day are not signal. This is a very opinionated version of design, of course, but it works for me. We want to eliminate this type of ornament because we want to focus on that which is useful.

So how do we actually do that? I have actually just three very simple principles that you can use in visual design. The first and most important is contrast. I'll walk you through a very simple example. The most basic type of contrast you can give is bold versus not bold—more important, less important. Incredibly simple. Bold is not the only way you can denote what is important versus not. You can use color, and that's an incredibly valuable tool. You can also use size. And so immediately you can kind of see, as a visual designer, I am being opinionated about what you should pay attention to, what is important, and what is less important.

And so what's funny is if we unwind those things, all of these suddenly become basically the same level. If everything is bold, nothing is bold. So that's something that you should definitely remember as you go about your business and you try to design anything. It's all of your choices around color, around weight—it really is a question of contrast. And so it goes both to the less important but also the more important. If you want to, you can use color and weight and size to also make something even more important.

The final really cool trick that I like to use is the squint test. So if you look at any webpage, any effective mobile app, immediately if you just squint your eyes at it, if you can't make out the details of what's on that page but you can just make out the basic shapes without having the ability to read any of this, you kind of know: oh, there's something. Your eyes are immediately drawn to the thing that is the highest contrast, the highest weight on the page. And so this is a very basic tool—basically your hammer for visual design is contrast.

Closeness and Proximity

The second principle is closeness equals relatedness. Things that are close together on a page are perceived as being related. This is an incredibly important principle that's surprisingly easy to get wrong.

The third principle is using a grid. A grid gives your design visual hierarchy and helps users scan and find things quickly.

When you combine contrast and closeness, you get a grid that works really well—users can find things quickly because related items are close together, important items have higher contrast, and the grid provides the overall structure.

Padding, Margin, and Layout

When it comes to borders and lines, there's a very simple hierarchy to follow. First, use padding and margin. Don't forget to use a grid to get the most out of your space. Whitespace can be good—you don't need to fill everything up all the time.

Basically, figure out what you need to put on the page, and then try to use padding and margin to the extent that you can. You can use a grid, you can put related things close to each other. Then next, use a line if you have to. So ninety percent of the time you can probably get away with just using a proper grid with enough spacing, putting related things together with good headings, thinking through the contrast.

But finally, a box is actually a very important thing. It draws a lot of attention, so that's why you'll see it so commonly on websites around a call to action. It really is a high contrast thing. But be very, very careful when you use it because otherwise you end up with this soup of a ton of boxes and you have no idea what's important and what you should be doing. Whereas if you use a grid, if you use this sort of visual hierarchy, it is very straightforward.

The other thing that's very valuable is you don't have to fill every single design margin to margin. White space can be incredibly useful. Sometimes it's even helpful in directing a user, helping to explain what's going on. Having space on the side just gives you a place to put that, or to focus the user's attention.

Those are incredibly basic, but a lot of these minimal techniques are really from the folks who created Helvetica, from Swiss design in the mid-twentieth century. These are incredibly simple things, but the use of contrast, the use of a grid, and the use of color can actually do a lot.

Wayfinding and Attention to Detail

Think about what you're looking at at every given point in an airport. What are you trying to do? What are your goals? And lack of proper signage is one of the things — I get lost in airports all the time. I got lost in Paris accidentally just like this weekend, actually.

And so this is actually what feedback is designed to solve.

There are actually two types that both designers and founders should be really, really aware of. One is usability testing. That moment between the wireframe — you can kind of even do this in parallel. Once you have the wireframe, you can actually start doing usability testing with the wireframe. You can print it out and actually sit down with people who've never seen it before and just sort of walk them through it. Or even better, just ask open-ended questions: what are we looking at? You can prompt them a little bit with, like, "oh, well, you're here to do blah blah blah," and it's like, "what are you reading? What are you going to click next? Why am I here?" Before you even write a line of code, you can do a lot to test out whether or not this thing is going to work. And it's way cheaper to do that than to actually have to fix it in code later.

And then the other part that is way, way underappreciated but is extremely important for great product is actually customer support. Most people treat their support line as basically like a shredder. They just don't even look at it. They don't reply to it. But the reality of product is that most — and this is an extremely, extremely common problem for people building things for other people — is that we always assume that the product that we're creating, well, it's just the visible part, right? It's the eighty percent case. It's the ideal case in your wireframe, in the requirement document.

But the best requirement documents, the best wireframes, the best designs, the best shipped thing actually solve all of these other use cases that a PM might sit there and say, "oh, well, once in a blue moon this happens." But they're actually ten thousand things like that that once in a blue moon happen. And if you add them all up, well, you have a broken product. And so that's one of the most fundamental reasons why we're so dissatisfied with the products that we use day to day. It's that some PM or some designer looked at that and said, "oh, when is that ever going to happen?"

So the devil truly is in these long tail bugs. As a founder, the best part is that these big companies don't think about this at all. They literally make it impossible for designers, engineers, and the product team to actually have anything to do with customer support. They just think of their users as cattle. The greatest advantage that you have in this room is that you're a human being. You built it yourself. When someone comes to you and says "oh my god, I'm stuck here, what happened?" you don't have to just throw that email in the garbage. You can say "oh my god, that's a bug. I'm gonna fix it." Once you do that, you have an evangelist for life because we live in a world with products that are incredibly impersonal. We're frustrated when our products don't work and nobody listens.

If you're the creator of that product and you listen well, you only need a couple hundred people like that, and you're well on your way to something that could be incredibly powerful. To steal a quote from Paul Bukai: don't try and go and make something that a thousand people kind of like. You really need to go and create something that a hundred people absolutely love. Customer support is the way you can do that.

The designer, the person who actually puts together the wireframes, who thinks through the user — they're the best on the whole team to actually think through that. They're awesome at advocating for the user. This process is incredibly complicated, but these are the parts of how I think about a great product. It's not just user research. It's all these things one after the other: usability testing and customer support are the key pieces of this. You're never done with just one sprint. You're basically in a perpetual cycle of doing this over and over again. You don't need people doing every single piece of this, but you do need to spend a little bit of time thinking about each piece of this.

The full cycle looks like this:

USER RESEARCH → PRODUCT DESIGN → INTERACTION DESIGN → VISUAL DESIGN → USABILITY TESTING → ENGINEERING / QA → CUSTOMER SUPPORT → REPEAT.

Great product equals ten thousand bugs fixed. And design knows how it should work — so they are the best advocate for the user.
